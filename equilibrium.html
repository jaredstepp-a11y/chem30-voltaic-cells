What is chemical equilibrium?

In a closed system, equilibrium is reached when the forward and reverse reaction rates become equal. Concentrations appear constant, but at the molecular level both reactions continue — this is why we call it dynamic equilibrium.

Why is it called "dynamic" equilibrium?▼

When a reaction first starts, the forward rate is fast (lots of reactants) and the reverse rate is zero (no products yet). As products build up, the forward rate slows and the reverse rate speeds up.

Eventually the two rates become equal. The concentrations of all species stop changing — they look "frozen." But the reactions have not stopped: molecules still react forward and backward at the same rate, so there is no net change.

Equilibrium is a balance of rates, not a stop. Think of a busy escalator: people step on and off constantly, but the number of people on it stays the same.
Writing the equilibrium constant expression (Kc)▼

The equilibrium constant expression is products over reactants, with each stoichiometric coefficient used as an exponent.

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:   Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ

Critical rule: only include aqueous ions (aq) and gases (g). Exclude pure solids (s) and pure liquids (l) — their concentrations don't change, so they're built into the constant.

Example: For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g): Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³)
What does the value of Kc tell you?▼

The size of Kc tells you which side of the reaction is favoured at equilibrium:

• Kc > 1 → products are favoured (equilibrium lies right)

• Kc < 1 → reactants are favoured (equilibrium lies left)

• Kc = 1 → neither strongly favoured

The single most important fact about Kc: only a change in temperature changes its value. Concentration and pressure changes shift the position of equilibrium, but Kc itself stays the same.
Common errors to avoid: (1) including solids or liquids in the Kc expression, (2) forgetting to use stoichiometric coefficients as exponents, and (3) thinking pressure or concentration changes affect the value of Kc — only temperature does.

Le Châtelier's Principle

When a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a stress (a change in concentration, temperature, or pressure), the equilibrium shifts in the direction that counteracts the stress and re-establishes balance.

Concentration changes▼

The system shifts away from whatever you add, and toward whatever you remove:

• Add a reactant → shift RIGHT (toward products)

• Remove a reactant → shift LEFT

• Add a product → shift LEFT (toward reactants)

• Remove a product → shift RIGHT

Think of the system trying to "undo" your change. Add too much of something and the reaction consumes it; take something away and the reaction replaces it.
Temperature changes▼

Treat heat as a reactant or product:

• Endothermic: heat is a reactant. Increasing temperature shifts RIGHT (favours forward).

• Exothermic: heat is a product. Increasing temperature shifts LEFT (favours reverse).

Temperature is the only stress that actually changes the value of Kc. All other stresses shift the position of equilibrium but leave Kc unchanged.
Pressure and volume changes (critical!)▼

Pressure changes only matter for gases. Count the moles of gas on each side:

• Reduce volume → increase pressure → shift toward fewer gas molecules

• Increase volume → decrease pressure → shift toward more gas molecules

• Add inert gas at constant volume → NO effect

• Add inert gas at constant pressure → shifts away from the gas added

Example: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) has 4 gas molecules left, 2 right. Increasing pressure shifts RIGHT, toward fewer molecules.

Equilibrium calculations with ICE tables

An ICE table organizes the math for finding equilibrium concentrations. ICE stands for Initial, Change, and Equilibrium — three rows that track how each concentration evolves.

Setting up an ICE table▼

I — Initial: the starting concentrations before any reaction (products usually start at 0).

C — Change: how much each concentration changes, written with the variable x and the stoichiometric ratios. Reactants decrease (−x), products increase (+x), each scaled by its coefficient.

E — Equilibrium: simply Initial + Change for each species.

You then substitute the Equilibrium row into the Kc expression and solve for x. A coefficient of 2 gives ±2x.
The approximation method (for weak acids & bases)▼

Solving the Kc expression often gives a quadratic. For weak acids and bases, we can usually avoid it with a shortcut.

The assumption: if x is very small compared to the initial concentration, then [HA] ≈ C₀ (initial concentration), simplifying the math.

When is it valid? Only if the initial concentration is at least 1000× larger than Ka (or Kb).

You MUST verify it: check that x is less than 5% of C₀. If (x / C₀) × 100% < 5%, the approximation is valid.

The 5% rule is non-negotiable on exams — always show the check. If x is more than 5% of C₀, the shortcut is invalid and you must use the quadratic formula.

3

Apply the approximation

Is 0.100 > 1000 × Ka = 0.018? Yes, so assume 0.100 − x ≈ 0.100.

1.8×10⁻⁵ = x² / 0.100
4

Solve for x = [H⁺]

x² = 1.8×10⁻⁶ → x = 1.34×10⁻³ mol/L

(1.34×10⁻³ / 0.100) × 100% = 1.34% < 5% ✓

Weak acids & bases

Strong acids and bases dissociate completely; weak acids and bases only partially dissociate, setting up an equilibrium. A handful of key relationships connect Ka, Kb, pH, and pOH.

The Ka, Kb, and Kw relationship▼

For a conjugate acid-base pair, Ka and Kb are linked by the ion product of water:

Ka × Kb = Kw   (at 25°C, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴)

So if you know Ka for an acid, you can find Kb for its conjugate base: Kb = Kw / Ka.

The stronger the acid (large Ka), the weaker its conjugate base (tiny Kb).
pH, pOH, and their relationship▼

pH and pOH are logarithmic measures of acidity and basicity:

pH = −log[H⁺]    pOH = −log[OH⁻]    pH + pOH = 14

To reverse the logarithm: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. A lower pH means a higher [H⁺] and a more acidic solution.

The scale is logarithmic — each whole pH unit is a 10× change in [H⁺]. A pH 3 solution has ten times the [H⁺] of a pH 4 solution.
Strong vs. weak — how to tell▼

Strong acids and bases dissociate essentially 100%. For a strong acid, [H⁺] equals the initial acid concentration directly — no equilibrium calculation needed.

Weak acids and bases only partially dissociate, so you need an ICE-table calculation with Ka or Kb.

Check your data booklet for the strong acid/base list. The six common strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄. If it isn't on the strong list, treat it as weak.

Acid-base titrations & buffers

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added. The shape of the curve — and the pH at the equivalence point — depends on whether the acid and base are strong or weak. (Chemistry 30 treats these qualitatively.)

Strong acid + strong base▼

When a strong acid is titrated with a strong base (or vice versa), the equivalence point occurs at pH = 7 (neutral). The salt formed (e.g. NaCl) does not hydrolyze.

The curve has a long, very steep vertical jump right at the equivalence point, passing through pH 7 at its midpoint.
Weak acid + strong base, and strong acid + weak base▼

Weak acid + strong base → equivalence point pH > 7 (basic). The conjugate base of the weak acid hydrolyzes, producing OH⁻.

Strong acid + weak base → equivalence point pH < 7 (acidic). The conjugate acid of the weak base hydrolyzes, producing H⁺.

A weak acid + strong base curve also has a flatter "buffer region" before the equivalence point, where the weak acid and its conjugate base are both present.
Buffer systems▼

A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.

Composition: a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base + conjugate acid) in roughly equal amounts.

Function: the weak acid neutralizes added base; the conjugate base neutralizes added acid — so pH stays nearly constant.

For Chemistry 30 you need to recognize a buffer system. Calculating the pH of a buffer is not required. The flat region of a weak acid titration curve is the buffer region.
