What is a voltaic cell?

A voltaic cell (galvanic cell) uses a spontaneous redox reaction to convert chemical energy into electrical energy. By physically separating the two half-reactions, electrons are forced to travel through a wire — that electron flow is usable electricity.

Why does splitting a redox reaction create electricity?▼

Drop zinc directly into copper sulfate and electrons transfer immediately — releasing energy as heat, not electricity. In a voltaic cell, the half-reactions are separated into different beakers. The only path for electrons is through the external wire, and that forced journey through the wire is exactly what we call electric current.

The voltage produced depends on how strongly the two species want to react. A very strong reducing agent paired with a very strong oxidizing agent creates a large potential difference and a high voltage.
What happens at the particle level?▼

Anode (Zn): Zinc atoms lose 2 electrons each, becoming Zn²⁺ ions that dissolve into solution. The electrode shrinks and loses mass over time.

Cathode (Cu): Cu²⁺ ions from solution gain 2 electrons, becoming solid Cu atoms that deposit on the electrode. The electrode grows heavier.

You can observe this directly: the zinc electrode gets thinner; the copper electrode accumulates a visible reddish copper coating.
Why is the salt bridge essential?▼

Without a salt bridge, the anode solution rapidly builds up positive charge (Zn²⁺ entering) while the cathode solution loses positive charge (Cu²⁺ removed). This charge imbalance stops electron flow almost immediately.

The salt bridge lets ions migrate: NO₃⁻ anions flow toward the anode; K⁺ cations flow toward the cathode.

The salt bridge carries ionic current; the wire carries electron current. Remove either and the circuit breaks — current stops instantly.

Analyzing a voltaic cell

A complete cell analysis requires five things: (1) write both half-reactions, (2) identify anode and cathode, (3) identify oxidizing and reducing agents, (4) write and balance the overall equation, and (5) express the cell in IUPAC cell notation.

Writing half-reactions: a step-by-step method▼

Step 1. Identify what is oxidized (oxidation state increases) and what is reduced (decreases). In Zn/Cu: Zn goes 0→+2 (oxidized); Cu²⁺ goes +2→0 (reduced).

Step 2. Write a skeleton equation then balance charge by adding electrons — on the RIGHT for oxidation, LEFT for reduction.

Oxidation: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻  (0 = 2+ − 2 ✓) Reduction: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu  (2+ − 2 = 0 ✓)
Always check: total charge on the left must equal total charge on the right of each half-reaction.
Oxidizing agent vs. reducing agent — clearing up the confusion▼

The oxidizing agent causes oxidation in something else by accepting electrons — so it is itself reduced.

The reducing agent causes reduction in something else by donating electrons — so it is itself oxidized.

In the Zn/Cu cell: Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent (accepts electrons, gets reduced). Zn is the reducing agent (donates electrons, gets oxidized).

Use OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons.

Combining half-reactions and IUPAC cell notation▼

Electrons lost must equal electrons gained. Multiply using LCM, add, then cancel electrons.

Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu ✓

IUPAC notation: anode left | phase boundary | salt bridge || cathode right.

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)
Never multiply E° values when scaling half-reactions — E° is intensive and stays constant regardless of coefficients.

Standard cell potential (E°cell)

The standard cell potential is the voltage produced when all species are at standard conditions. It predicts spontaneity and quantifies the driving force of the reaction, calculated from tabulated standard reduction potentials.

What exactly are "standard conditions"?▼

Standard state (°) is a defined baseline: all dissolved species at 1 mol/L, temperature at 25°C, all gases at 1 atm, pure solids/liquids assumed pure.

Real cells rarely operate at standard conditions — concentrations drop as the cell discharges. E° gives a reliable reference; actual voltage differs as conditions change (the Nernst equation describes this — an extension topic).
Why doesn't E°cell change when you multiply the equation?▼

E° is an intensive property — it measures driving force per electron, not total energy. Whether you write Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu or 2Cu²⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2Cu, E° stays +0.34 V. Never multiply E° values when balancing.

Contrast with ΔG° = −nFE°: total energy does scale with n. More electrons = more total energy, but the same voltage.
Connecting E°cell to spontaneity and Gibbs energy▼

ΔG° = −nFE°cell links voltage to thermodynamics:

• E°cell > 0 → spontaneous → voltaic cell (produces electricity)

• E°cell < 0 → non-spontaneous → requires electrical input (electrolysis)

• E°cell = 0 → system is at equilibrium

For Chemistry 30: positive E°cell = spontaneous. That's the key rule.

YouTube Watch: Calculating Standard Cell Potential

The formula

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Both E° values come from the reduction table as written. The subtraction handles the anode direction — never flip the sign manually.

Worked example: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

1

Read the cell notation

Left of || = anode (Zn). Right of || = cathode (Cu).

2

Look up E° values

E°cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu = +0.34 VE°anode: Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn = −0.76 V
3

Apply the formula

E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V ✓
4

Interpret

Positive E°cell → spontaneous. The cell delivers 1.10 V. This is the classic Daniell cell.

The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

Every E° value in the reduction table was measured relative to the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE), assigned 0.00 V by international convention. Understanding why a reference is needed and how to use the table to predict reactions is the core skill here.

Why can't we measure absolute electrode potentials?▼

A voltmeter always measures the potential difference between two electrodes — never the absolute potential of one alone. Think of measuring altitude: you need sea level = 0 m as a reference. Scientists chose the SHE as the reference and assigned it exactly 0.00 V.

Positive E° means the half-reaction is reduced more easily than H⁺. Negative E° means less easily than H₂.
How to predict spontaneous reactions using the table▼

Rule: A reaction is spontaneous if the oxidizing agent has a higher E° than the reducing agent. Electrons flow "downhill" from low E° to high E°.

Will Zn react with Cu²⁺?
Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V (higher → oxidizer) · Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V (lower → reducer)
+0.34 > −0.76 → Spontaneous ✓   E°cell = +1.10 V
Will Cu react with Zn²⁺?
Zn²⁺ would need the higher E° to be the oxidizer — but it's lower. Non-spontaneous ✗
Connection to the activity series▼

The E° table is a quantitative version of the activity series. The most reactive metals (K, Na, Mg, Al, Zn…) appear at the bottom of the E° table with the most negative E° — they are the strongest reducing agents.

Fluorine (F₂, +2.87 V) is the strongest oxidizing agent known. Lithium (Li, −3.05 V) is the strongest reducing agent. Displacement reactions from earlier chemistry are just spontaneous redox reactions where the lower-E° metal displaces the higher-E° metal from solution.

Hydrogen is the most abundant element and naturally has the lowest energy state
